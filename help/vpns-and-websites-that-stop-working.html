Why a VPN can break websites, and how to fix it

A virtual private network, or VPN, sends your traffic through an encrypted tunnel to a server run by the VPN provider, and websites see that server’s address instead of yours. This is useful for privacy on public Wi-Fi and for reaching services from other regions. It also explains many “this site does not work for me” moments.

What a VPN changes

  • Your apparent location. Sites see the VPN server’s country and city, not yours.
  • Your address. It is shared with many other users of the same server.
  • The route. Your traffic takes a detour through the VPN server, which adds distance and can add delay.
  • Your DNS. Many VPNs also use their own name resolvers.

Why sites misbehave over a VPN

The address is on a block list

VPN addresses are shared, so if someone else on the same server behaves badly, the address can be flagged. Banks, shops, streaming services and security filters often block or challenge known VPN ranges. You may see an access denied message, a repeating human-check, or a page that never loads. Our guide to the 403 forbidden error explains one common form.

Too many requests from one address

Many people sharing one address can together trip rate limits that no single user would. See what a 429 error means.

Location mismatches

If your account, payment card or device says one country and the VPN says another, some services will ask for extra verification or lock the account until you confirm it is really you.

Slower speeds and higher latency

Every extra mile adds delay. Choosing a server far from you, or a busy one, can make browsing sluggish, video buffer and games lag. See latency, packet loss and jitter.

Broken connections after a drop

If the VPN connection drops and your device sends traffic outside the tunnel, or a kill switch blocks everything, sites may appear to stop loading entirely until the tunnel is restored.

DNS conflicts

A VPN’s resolver can disagree with your usual one, so a name may resolve differently or fail. See what DNS is and why it fails.

A quick way to tell if the VPN is the cause

  1. Disconnect the VPN and try the site again.
  2. If it now works, the VPN or its address was the problem.
  3. Connect again and pick a different server, ideally in your own country.
  4. If a particular site still blocks you, contact the VPN provider and the site, and consider using the VPN only when you need it.

Fixes to try

  • Change server. A different server means a different address with a different history.
  • Change protocol. Most VPN apps offer several connection methods, and one may work better on a restrictive network.
  • Use split tunnelling, if your app offers it, so only chosen apps go through the VPN and everything else uses your normal connection.
  • Update the app. Old versions can have bugs and may use outdated settings.
  • Flush DNS after connecting or disconnecting. See how to flush your DNS cache.
  • Check the clock. A wrong date and time breaks secure connections.

Why our checker may say “blocked”

Automated tools, including ours, can run into the same kind of filters, since our probes also come from shared addresses in data centres. When a site answers our check with a challenge page rather than the real page, we report Blocked and explain it does not mean the site is down. See why some sites block automated checks.

VPN and privacy: what it does and does not do

  • It hides your traffic from the local network, which is helpful on public Wi-Fi.
  • It hides your address from the sites you visit.
  • It does not make you anonymous. The VPN provider can see your traffic, and sites can still recognise you from accounts, cookies and browser details.
  • Choose a provider whose privacy practices you have read and understood.

Summary

If a site misbehaves, turn the VPN off and try again. If that fixes it, change server or settings, or use the VPN only where it is needed. Most VPN problems come down to a blocked shared address, a distant server or a DNS mismatch.
