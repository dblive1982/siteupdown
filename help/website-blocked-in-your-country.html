Why a website may be blocked in your country, and how to tell

Sometimes a website works for friends overseas but not for you. It may not be broken at all. It can be blocked on your network, restricted in your country, or set to refuse visitors from your region. This guide explains the different kinds of blocking, how to tell them apart from a real outage, and what is sensible to do about each.

Down, or blocked?

Start by establishing whether the site works anywhere else. Paste the address into the website checker, which tests from our server, and look at the outage map, which shows how sites respond from fifteen places around the world. If a site loads from other countries but not from yours, or fails only on your connection, you are probably dealing with a block or a regional restriction, not an outage. See also why a site can be down for some people and not others.

The main kinds of blocking

  • Network blocking by the place you are connected. Schools, workplaces, libraries and public Wi-Fi often filter categories such as social media, gaming or streaming. If the site works on mobile data or at home, this is the cause. See works on mobile data but not Wi-Fi.
  • Internet provider filters. Some providers apply filters, sometimes by default and sometimes because a court has ordered certain sites to be blocked. Many let you review or switch off optional filters in your account.
  • Country-level restrictions. A government may require providers to block particular sites or services, either temporarily or permanently. This is a legal and political matter, and the experience varies a great deal between countries and over time.
  • The site restricting you. Some websites deliberately refuse visitors from certain countries, because of licensing, law, or security policy. The site, not your network, makes that choice, and it usually shows a message such as “not available in your region” or “access denied”. See error 403 forbidden.
  • Security services in front of the site. Some sites sit behind protection that blocks addresses with a poor reputation. Shared networks, some VPNs and compromised devices can get caught by this.

What different symptoms suggest

  • A page that says the site is blocked, restricted or filtered comes from your network or provider.
  • A message from the site itself about your region means the site is restricting access.
  • A connection that simply times out or resets is ambiguous. It might be an outage, a firewall or a block. Compare with another connection, and see connection timed out.
  • A name that does not resolve may be caused by DNS filtering by your provider or network. Our DNS lookup shows what the name should return, and you can compare with what your device gets.

What is reasonable to do

  1. Check with another connection. Try mobile data, or another network, to learn whether the block is tied to where you are.
  2. Look at your provider or network settings. Filters that are optional can be changed in your account or by asking the administrator.
  3. Contact the site. If the site is refusing your region, its support pages usually explain why and which countries are affected.
  4. Think about the rules before using a workaround. A VPN can change where a site thinks you are, and many people use one for privacy. But bypassing a restriction can break a service’s terms of use, and in some countries or workplaces it is against the rules. Read why a VPN can break websites, and make an informed decision about the risks in your situation.

If you run a website

If visitors in one country report they cannot reach you, test from that region. The outage map and regional checks help. Look at any country or address rules in your security settings, and check that a protection service is not treating a whole network as hostile. Tell visitors plainly when you restrict access, and why, so they are not left guessing.

We report whether our own checks can reach a site. We cannot say why a particular country blocks a site, and we make no claims about the laws of any place.
