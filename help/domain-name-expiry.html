What happens when a domain name expires?

A domain name is rented, not bought. It is registered for a set period, usually one year, and has to be renewed. If it is not, the website and email attached to it stop working, and eventually somebody else can register it.

The usual sequence

Exact rules differ between registrars and endings such as .com or .co.uk, but the pattern is similar:

  1. Expiry day. The registration runs out. Many registrars switch the site off, or point it at a parked page.
  2. Grace period (often up to 30 to 45 days). You can usually still renew at the normal price.
  3. Redemption period (often around 30 days). The domain is held back, and getting it restored is possible but costs a lot more.
  4. Release. The domain is deleted and becomes available for anyone to register. Domain traders watch for good names at this stage.

What stops working

  • The website, because the name no longer points to your server.
  • Email at that domain: messages bounce, or go to whoever holds the name next.
  • Anything that signs in using the domain, such as some apps and verification links.

How to avoid it

  • Turn on auto-renew, and keep a valid payment card on file.
  • Check the contact email on the registration. Renewal reminders go there, and an old address means you never see them.
  • Register for several years if you plan to keep it.
  • Check the expiry date regularly. Every check on siteupdown.com shows when the domain registration ends, so you can look up your own domain in seconds.

If it has already lapsed

Contact your registrar immediately and ask about the grace and redemption periods. Do not wait: each stage costs more, and the final one cannot be reversed.

What happens when a domain expires

Domains are rented, usually yearly. When the rent is not paid the domain goes through stages, and the details vary by ending (.com, .co.uk and so on):

  1. Expiry day: the site often stops working, or shows a registrar’s parking page, but you can still renew at the normal price.
  2. Grace period: typically up to about 30 days, in which renewing is still allowed, sometimes with a late fee.
  3. Redemption period: roughly another 30 days, where recovery is possible but costs much more.
  4. Release: the domain is deleted and anyone can register it, including someone who may misuse the old name.

Why it is dangerous

Email on the domain stops working at the same time as the website. Search rankings, links and customers’ trust built over years can be lost, and a competitor or spammer may pick up the name.

How to prevent it

  • Turn on auto-renew and keep a valid card on file.
  • Use an email address you check for registrar reminders, not a past employee’s.
  • Renew for several years at once for important domains.
  • Put the expiry date in a calendar with a reminder a month ahead.

A worked example

A card expires, auto-renew fails, and the reminder goes to an old inbox. The site goes offline on expiry day and the owner assumes the host has failed. A check for the domain shows a lookup failure (see NXDOMAIN), and the registrar’s dashboard shows the real cause. Paying within the grace period restores the site once DNS settles (DNS propagation).
