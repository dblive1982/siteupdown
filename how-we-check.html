How we check.

A status checker is only useful if you can trust it. This page explains, step by step, how we reach a result, and where we choose to say “not sure” instead of guessing.

1. Our own server asks first

When you check an address, our server requests the page over a secure connection, follows any redirects, and records the status code and how long it took. If nothing answers, we also try the other form of the address (with or without “www”), because some sites only reply on one. We only fetch public web addresses.

2. Fifteen probes ask too

At the same moment, probes on the public Globalping network request the same page from fifteen countries across six continents: the United States, Canada, Mexico, Brazil, Argentina, the United Kingdom, Germany, France, South Africa, the United Arab Emirates, India, Singapore, Japan, South Korea and Australia. If one is unavailable we use a nearby place, and we only show the places that really answered.

3. What each result means

ResultWhat it means
UpThe page answered normally.
SlowIt answered, but took longer than usual.
DownOur server got server errors (such as 500, 502 or 503) on repeated attempts, or a site that had been answering stopped, and probes in other countries could not reach it either.
No answerNothing replied. The site may be down, or it may be ignoring automated checks. We do not call this down.
Not sureThe site replied with a block or limit (such as 403 or 429), so we cannot tell what a real visitor sees.
Collecting dataA new history graph needs a few checks before it can show anything honest.

4. We avoid false alarms

  • A site is only recorded as down after repeated failures, not a single bad moment.
  • Before we record an outage, we ask probes in other countries. If any of them can reach the site, it is our server that is being blocked, so we do not call it down.
  • Large sites often ignore automated requests. We say so, and link you to the site so you can try it yourself.
  • Our checker says who it is. We do not pretend to be a browser to get around a site’s protection.

5. How often we check

  • Popular sites listed on our status pages are re-checked about every five minutes.
  • Sites on a watchlist are re-checked about every minute.
  • The live graph samples about every ten seconds while someone is watching it, and slows down if the site looks stressed.
  • The worldwide picture on each status page is refreshed in the background, a few sites at a time, so it can be a few hours old. Its age is shown.
  • Certificates and domain dates are checked about once a day.

6. Problem reports from visitors

On each status page, visitors can report that a site is not working. Reports are anonymous and unverified, and each person can report once every 30 minutes. We show them as counts over time, so you can see whether many people are affected, but we never treat reports as proof of an outage.

7. What we cannot know

  • A result is a snapshot of one moment, from our servers and the probes, not from your network.
  • A site that works for the probes can still fail for you because of your connection, DNS, browser or device. A site that fails for the probes can still work for you.
  • We test the page address, not every feature. A site can be reachable while its login, app or payments are broken.
